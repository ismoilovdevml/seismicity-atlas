Aftershock productivity and Båth's law

Båth's law says the largest aftershock is typically about 1.2 magnitude units smaller than the mainshock, and the Reasenberg-Jones model combines the Omori-Utsu decay with the Gutenberg-Richter law to give how many aftershocks of each size to expect.

Simple explanation

Big earthquakes have big aftershocks, but usually clearly smaller ones. Markus Båth noticed that the gap is surprisingly stable, about 1.2 on average, with a wide spread from one sequence to the next.

How many aftershocks a sequence produces is its productivity. Put together how fast aftershocks die out (Omori-Utsu) and how their sizes are spread (Gutenberg-Richter) and you can say, for a sequence that has been fitted, how many events above a given size the model implies in a time window. This is the Reasenberg-Jones model that agencies use for aftershock advice.

In this atlas these numbers describe past sequences only. They are not a forecast.

An everyday picture

A big firework has a crackling tail: many small sparks, a few larger ones, and the largest spark is usually clearly smaller than the main burst.

Technical details

Båth's law: ΔM = Mmain − Mlargest aftershock ≈ 1.2as an average with a spread of several tenths. The atlas gap uses magnitudes from the event layer (ISC-GEM Mw or ComCat as served, converted where needed), so it is only indicative.

Reasenberg-Jones rate of aftershocks with magnitude ≥ M at time t (days):λ(t, M) = K · 10^(−b (M − Mcat)) · (t + c)^−pwith K, c, p from the Omori-Utsu fit above Mcat and b the sequence's b-positive. The expected number in a window is the integral over t, and the chance of at least one event is 1 − e^(−N) for a Poisson process (Reasenberg & Jones 1989).

Productivity scales with mainshock size; Page et al. (2016) give global, region-dependent parameters for operational aftershock forecasts. The atlas's fits are retrospective and teleseismic (M ≥ 4.5 completeness), so they say nothing about small aftershocks or about ongoing sequences.

The upcoming Sequence Monitor reuses these quantities for recent sequences; the same caveat applies there.
