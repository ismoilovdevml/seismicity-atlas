Magnitude types

Earthquake “magnitude” is not one number but a family of measurements, each made on different seismic waves, and only the moment magnitude Mw keeps growing for the largest earthquakes.

Interactive diagram · Real example from the atlas →

Simple explanation

A magnitude is read from the shaking that seismometers record. Different scales listen to different waves: mb uses short, about one-second body waves; Ms uses slower surface waves of about twenty seconds; ML (the original Richter idea) and Md (from how long the record lasts) are made by local networks for nearby, mostly small events.

Mw, the moment magnitude, is different: it comes from the seismic moment, the size of the fault area times how far it slipped times the stiffness of the rock. It does not run out of range.

The short-wave scales stop growing for big earthquakes. A great earthquake and a merely large one can get almost the same mb. This is called saturation.

An everyday picture

Measuring a wave on the beach with a one-metre ruler works for small waves. For a tsunami you still read “one metre”. mb is the short ruler; Mw measures the whole volume of water.

Technical details

Seismic moment and moment magnitude (Hanks–Kanamori form, M₀ in N·m):M₀ = μ · A · D, Mw = (2/3) · (log₁₀ M₀ − 9.1)where μ is the shear modulus, A the rupture area and D the average slip. Because Mw follows the moment itself, it grows without limit.

mb is measured on P waves with periods near 1 s and Ms on Rayleigh waves near 20 s. Once the rupture is much longer than those wavelengths, the amplitude at that period hardly grows any more; mb flattens around the low 6s and Ms around 8 (exact levels depend on the agency and formula).

In this atlas: b is computed only on ISC-GEM Mw (1976–2021). ComCat's preferred magnitude is mostly mb for M 4.5–6 and Mw above, so a b-value fitted on ComCat as served depends on where the type switches. ComCat-only events are put on a common scale only for the event layer, with fixed conversions (Scordilis 2006 slopes): Mw = 0.92 + 0.85 mb, and a bilinear Ms relation. The event tooltip names the magnitude type of each dot.

Limitations: conversions between scales carry a scatter of about 0.2 magnitude units and bias b when used for statistics (Castellaro et al. 2006). Local ML and Md scales differ from network to network, which is why regional layers are kept apart.
